Foam Chemistry, Explained From The Molecule Up

Two liquids meet. Ninety seconds later, it's a solid full of gas.

Polyurethane foam isn't poured or carved — it's grown. A polyol and an isocyanate are mixed, and two reactions race each other: one builds the polymer, the other inflates it. What survives the race is the foam in your mattress, your seat cushion, or your attic insulation.

The linkage that gives polyurethane its name R–N=C=O + R'–OH → R–NH–C(=O)–O–R'

01 — The Two Reactions

Gelling builds the skeleton. Blowing inflates it.

Every polyurethane foam formulation runs two competing reactions at once. Neither alone makes foam — one without the other gives you a solid brick or a puddle of gas-filled liquid.

Gelling reaction

Polyol + isocyanate → urethane

R–N=C=O + R'–OH → R–NH–CO–O–R'

The isocyanate's –N=C=O group attacks the polyol's hydroxyl, forming the urethane (carbamate) linkage. Chains stitch together into a polymer network — this is what gives the foam its structure and load-bearing strength.

Blowing reaction

Water + isocyanate → CO₂ + urea

R–N=C=O + H₂O → R–NH₂ + CO₂↑
R–NH₂ + R'–N=C=O → R–NH–CO–NH–R'

Water reacts with isocyanate to form an unstable carbamic acid, which decomposes into an amine and CO₂ gas. That gas is the bubble. The amine goes on to react with more isocyanate, adding urea linkages into the polymer.

02 — What Tunes The Race

Catalysts decide which reaction wins, and when.

Left alone, the two reactions run at whatever rate the raw chemistry allows — usually too slow and too uneven to make usable foam. Catalysts and surfactants are how a formulator steers the outcome.

Amine catalysts

e.g. triethylenediamine — accelerate blowing

Tertiary amines preferentially speed up the water–isocyanate reaction, generating CO₂ earlier and faster. Too much, and the foam inflates before the polymer network is strong enough to hold the gas — it collapses.

Organotin catalysts

e.g. stannous octoate — accelerate gelling

Tin catalysts speed up the polyol–isocyanate reaction instead, building the polymer network faster. Too much relative to blowing, and the foam sets before it can fully rise — it stays dense and small.

Silicone surfactants

polyether-modified siloxanes

These lower surface tension at the gas–liquid interface, so CO₂ bubbles nucleate small and evenly instead of merging into large voids. They stabilize the cell walls just long enough for the polymer to set around them.

The balance point

gelling rate vs. blowing rate

Every commercial formulation is really a bet on timing: the gel reaction must catch up to the blowing reaction at almost exactly the moment the foam finishes rising — not before, not after.

03 — The Rise

What a block of foam does in its first five minutes

Watch a foam pour and you're watching the balance above play out in real time. These are typical stages for a water-blown flexible slabstock foam — exact timings shift with formulation, but the sequence doesn't.

Mixing 0 s

Polyol blend, isocyanate, catalysts, surfactant, and water are combined under high shear. The mixture is still a clear, low-viscosity liquid.

Cream time ~8–15 s

The liquid turns opaque and pale as the first CO₂ bubbles nucleate throughout the mixture — the "cream" moment gives this stage its name.

Rise time ~60–120 s

CO₂ generation peaks. The foam expands to its full volume as the exotherm from both reactions pushes the core temperature toward 130–150 °C.

Tack-free time ~120–180 s

The gelling reaction has built enough polymer network that the surface stops sticking to the touch. A skin forms over the still-curing interior.

Cure hours – days

Crosslinking continues long after the foam looks finished. Full mechanical properties — compression set, tensile strength — develop over the following 24–72 hours.

05 — Cell Structure

Whether the walls between bubbles survive decides everything

As CO₂ cells expand and press against each other, their thin polymer walls either rupture or hold. That single mechanical outcome splits all polyurethane foam into two families.

Open-cell

Cell walls rupture during rise, leaving an interconnected, air-permeable lattice.

  • Softer, more compressible
  • Breathes — air and moisture pass through
  • Lower density, lower R-value
  • Mattresses, cushions, acoustic foam

Closed-cell

Cell walls stay intact, sealing gas inside discrete pockets.

  • Rigid, higher compressive strength
  • Resists moisture and airflow
  • Higher density, higher R-value
  • Spray insulation, structural panels, buoyancy foam

06 — Where It Ends Up

One chemistry, five very different products

Formulation — polyol functionality, isocyanate type, catalyst ratio, blowing agent — determines which of these a given batch becomes.

Flexible slabstock Mattress cores, furniture cushioning
Molded flexible Car seats, headrests, armrests
Rigid board & spray Wall and roof insulation, refrigeration panels
Integral skin Steering wheels, shoe midsoles, armrests
A note on isocyanates: MDI and TDI, the diisocyanates behind most polyurethane foam, are respiratory sensitizers. Industrial production and any DIY spray-foam work require proper ventilation, respiratory protection, and skin protection — sensitization can develop with repeated exposure even at low levels.
